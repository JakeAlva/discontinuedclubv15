Maui Burst vs Baja Gold: two pineapple Dews, two different stories.

One grew out of a retailer exclusive. The other joined a summer Baja lineup. A shared fruit flavor does not make the releases interchangeable.

By Discontinued Club ResearchPublished November 19, 20266 minute read
The short answer

Same broad flavor family does not mean the same product.

Maui Burst and Baja Gold are separately named pineapple-flavored Mountain Dew releases. Maui Burst began as a fall 2019 limited offering before a 2020 year-round Dollar General announcement; Baja Gold was introduced in the 2022 Summer of Baja lineup. Our listings are older collectible stock in different formats: a 16 oz Maui Burst can and a 20 oz Baja Gold bottle.

Maui Burst: the retailer-exclusive route

PepsiCo's January 21, 2020 announcement says Maui Burst followed a limited fall 2019 offering. The company announced a year-round Dollar General exclusive beginning February 1, 2020, in 16 oz single cans, with pineapple as the added flavor. That gives us a documented starting point for the format, not proof of every later package or retailer arrangement.

The retailer connection matters because a shopping memory can be tied to a place as much as a taste. Someone may remember finding the can during an ordinary store visit rather than during a nationally advertised summer event. In a collection, that is a different kind of story from a release built around a seasonal lineup.

Our interpretation is that the format is part of the appeal: the tall single can is a distinct object, not merely a generic container for pineapple soda. Still, recognizing the format does not date an individual example. The 2020 description attached to our listing has not been independently matched to a readable printed code in this article.

Baja Gold: a place in the 2022 summer lineup

PepsiCo's June 7, 2022 Summer of Baja announcement introduced Baja Gold and Baja Mango Gem alongside returning Baja Blast and Baja Blast Zero Sugar. Gold was the pineapple entry; Mango Gem was the mango entry. PepsiCo described the seasonal selection as a limited-time nationwide retail offering.

That context makes Gold more than a color variation in a search result. Its name places it within a particular family of releases. A collector building a Baja shelf may care about that relationship even if someone shopping by fruit flavor would put it beside Maui Burst instead.

The timeline also corrects a common listing problem. A seller-entered year is not stronger evidence than a dated manufacturer announcement introducing the product. We removed an unverified 2021 claim from our website's Baja Gold listing. That does not mean we can now certify the bottle's exact production date as 2022; the individual code still needs verification.

Does pineapple mean they taste the same?

No reliable conclusion about identical taste follows from the shared word pineapple. A broad flavor description does not disclose the complete formula, proportions or the experience of drinking it. The sources establish that these are two separately marketed products; they do not give us a controlled comparison of the liquids.

We have not tasted these two items side by side, so this is not a ranking. Claims such as one being more tart, sweeter or more candy-like would need evidence we do not have. Nor would comments about a freshly purchased release necessarily describe an older collectible container years later.

For someone chasing a remembered favorite, the best first step is therefore identification. Was it Maui Burst, Baja Gold, a particular container, or simply a yellow pineapple-themed Dew? Look for a photograph or a saved purchase detail before assuming another pineapple release will recreate the same memory. Similarity is a starting point for a question, not the answer.

The actual comparison: a 16 oz can and a 20 oz bottle

Our two listings differ in an immediately measurable way. The Maui Burst item is a 16 oz can. The Baja Gold item is a 20 oz bottle, which contains four more fluid ounces, or 25% more labeled volume. This is a comparison of the offered formats, not a statement that every Maui Burst or Baja Gold release used those sizes.

For display purposes, consider the space each occupies, how the artwork wraps around the container, and whether you prefer cans or bottles in that part of your collection. There is no need to invent a universal winner: a set built around a single container type has a different purpose from one tracing a flavor family across different packaging.

For a purchase comparison, look at the delivered total and what one listing unit includes. A price per ounce can be calculated, but it will not capture label condition or how important a particular edition is to you. These listings are individual containers, and buying one does not include the other or create a mixed pack.

Three dates that should never be collapsed into one

The announcement date tells you when a company described a release or distribution change. It is evidence about product history. It does not show when the container in a seller's photograph was filled.

The printed package date or code belongs to the physical item. Ask for a clear photograph rather than assuming that a title year is a transcription of the marking. If the code cannot be confidently interpreted, leave that uncertainty visible instead of choosing a convenient year.

The listing date is when an item was offered for sale. A newly listed collectible is not necessarily newly manufactured. This matters especially when a search engine presents a recent update beside an old product photograph: recency of the webpage and recency of the contents are separate questions.

For our current stock, the printed dates remain unverified in this guide. We are describing older collectibles, not fresh tasting samples. Full and unopened should not be read as a freshness guarantee or proof that an aged beverage is suitable to drink.

A historical permanent announcement is not a live stock feed

It is tempting to treat the words permanent and limited-time as opposites that settle every future question. They are better read in the context of when the statement was made. A dated plan for continued distribution is not a promise that every size stays unchanged indefinitely, just as a limited promotion does not establish a precise day when the final resale listing disappears.

This article does not establish a current nationwide production verdict for every Maui Burst format, or guarantee that either product can be found at a particular chain today. Its purpose is to compare documented origins and the specific older items we offer. That narrower answer is more useful than turning an old press release into a current store-locator claim.

The same distinction applies to our own stock. The number on a product page is the quantity available online, not a census of every can or bottle elsewhere. We leave exact counts out of the journal so readers do not encounter a months-old inventory promise while researching the history.

Which one belongs in your collection?

Start with the collection you actually want. For a shelf organized around store-exclusive history or a tall-can format, the Maui Burst listing may be the relevant one. For a Baja-focused shelf or a bottle collection, the Baja Gold listing is the closer match. Those are organizing suggestions, not taste or investment recommendations.

Before ordering, ask about the actual container's condition and date. If it is intended as a gift, make sure the recipient wants an older collectible rather than an ordinary grocery drink. If you are trying to replace something you remember, compare the full name and format before paying.

The interesting part of this pairing is not deciding which pineapple label wins. It is seeing how two products can share a flavor category while carrying different histories and physical identities. Knowing those differences makes a collection more intentional and a purchase less dependent on guesswork.
